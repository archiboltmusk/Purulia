How to adopt a spot

1. Stand thereGo to the spot you will look after: your shop front, school gate, the corner by your club.
2. Give a public nameOpen Adopt a spot and type the name people know you by. Your account stays private.
3. Bring a partnerPick your role. A shop and a para club can share one spot: the second one standing there joins the first.
4. Keep it cleanAny report within 50 m shows against your spot until it is fixed. One person can look after up to 3 spots.

For municipalities and ward offices: an Adopt-a-Spot partnership

A proposal, not a scheme that exists today. Parishkar has no agreement with any municipality. A ward office that wants to try it can use this page as the public record.

Sweeping a corner alone does not keep it clean; it fills up again. A spot stays clean when three parties each own one part of it:

Ward officeClears the spot once (silt, garbage, bleaching powder and lime), paints the wall, adds a light and lidded bins, and empties those bins every morning without fail.
Shop or tea stallThe daytime anchor. Keeps the bins beside the shop, asks customers not to drop cups and wrappers, and reports anyone dumping.
Para clubThe social anchor. Keeps an eye out at night and early morning, and looks after the walls, art and planters.

How to start

  1. Pick the spots. The councillor and sanitary inspector choose the ward's worst black spots. The map's list of spots that keep filling up can help. Meet the club secretary and the shops next to it at the spot itself.
  2. Day zero. Deep clean, lime and paint. Local youth or art students paint the walls. A light removes the dark corner; heavy planters stop bags being thrown from bikes.
  3. Close the loop. Put covered bins beside the shop, not loose on the corner. Tell the morning tipper to empty these bins first. If pickup fails for two days, the shop will give up, and the spot is a dump again.
  4. Watch and ask. The club takes turns at the spot in the early morning and points people with bags to the door-to-door tipper. A word from a shopkeeper to a regular customer carries weight a fine does not.
  5. Put it on the record. The shop and club adopt the spot here, standing at it, each in their role. Send the ward office's written commitment (a letter or order) to the moderators; once checked, it shows against the spot with its source. Reports within 50 m then show whether the spot is holding.

Ideas a municipality could offer

None of these is current policy anywhere we know of. They are ideas to discuss:

  • For shops and vendors: a small trade-licence renewal rebate, or waived commercial waste charges, for keeping the frontage clean; two branded lidded bins (dry and wet); protection from sudden eviction drives for vendors who keep their zone clean.
  • For clubs: sports-equipment or ward grants linked to how the spot holds up; a permanent sign, "Maintained in partnership with [club] and Ward No. X"; a yearly cleanliness award given at Durga Puja.

What makes it fail

  • Open masonry vats. A concrete enclosure invites bulk dumping. Use lidded bins with handles and wheels.
  • One faction owning the spot. Rotate credit across local groups so it stays everyone's.
  • Missed pickups. The whole partnership rests on the ward office emptying the bins every day. A report of an overflowing bin at an adopted spot is the ward office's to fix, not the shop's.
